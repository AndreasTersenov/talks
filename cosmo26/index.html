The non-Gaussian information and the worst small-scale systematic live on the same scales

To be able to trust our HOS results we must investigate how they are affected by each systematic at the contour level.

question 1

How much does unmodelled feedback bias our higher-order statistics, and how does that grow with survey area?

question 2

Once those scales are cut, is there any constraining power left to gain over the power spectrum?

SAY: higher-order statistics are usually demonstrated on clean simulations; Stage IV is systematics-limited, so what decides whether we can use them is how they behave under contamination. Baryonic feedback - AGN and supernovae - pushes gas out of haloes and suppresses matter on small scales, mimicking a cosmological signal, exactly where the constraining power lives and where the feedback models disagree most with each other. Point at the figure: both the extra information and the contamination live at small scales. Now, the conservative response is to throw the affected scales away. The question is how much that costs. OPTIMISTIC: only the smallest scales are affected, we cut them, and most of the non-Gaussian information is still there. CLICK. PESSIMISTIC: the contamination reaches much further, and once you cut it there is nothing beyond-Gaussian left worth having - the power spectrum would do just as well. Which of these is true is an empirical question, and it is the first thing we measured.

§1 Wavelet peak counts and the $\ell_1$-norm are 1-pt statistics on the same starlet decomposition

wavelet peak counts

local maxima of the SNR field, counted per SNR bin → focus only on a subset of the field

starlet ℓ1-norm
$$\ell_1^{\,j,i} \;=\; \sum_{u=1}^{\#\mathcal{S}_{j,i}} \big| \mathcal{S}_{j,i}[u] \big| \;=\; \lVert \mathcal{S}_{j,i} \rVert_1$$

$\mathcal{S}_{j,i}$ — the coefficients of scale $j$ whose SNR falls in bin $i$

sum of absolute coefficients per band and SNR bin → encodes information from every pixel (peaks, voids, ...)

SAY: both of our higher-order statistics are ONE-POINT statistics - histograms of pixel values - and both are made multi-scale by the same starlet transform, which writes the map as a sum of band-pass images, each carrying structure of a characteristic angular size (adjacent bands overlap - they are band-passes, not a partition). That matters twice: each band covers a different frequency range so the covariance is nearly diagonal, and a systematic confined to small scales lands in ONE band, which is what makes the cheap scale cut possible later. Peaks: count local maxima of the SNR field per SNR bin - simple and well established, but it keeps only the discrete features. The l1-norm generalises it: sum the absolute coefficients in each SNR bin instead of counting maxima, so every pixel contributes, voids as well as peaks, and there is no feature-detection step to define or tune. That is our analytical hero for the rest of the talk. CAREFUL: the figure labels its bands 1.2-9.4 arcmin (thesis illustration at a finer pixel scale); OUR analysis uses the four finest dyadic bands, roughly 10 to 80 arcmin, and drops j=1. Do not quote the figure's numbers out loud.

§1 Baryonic Bias Scales with Survey Area

  • At 14,000 deg² (Stage IV): $C_\ell$ shows 2.2σ, peaks and the $\ell_1$-norm 3.6σ
  • At full sky: $C_\ell$ reaches $\sim3.5\sigma$, both HOS exceed 6σ
  • HOS show higher bias than $C_\ell$: more sensitive to the baryonically-contaminated small scales

measured at full map resolution: $\ell_{\rm max}=1024$ for $C_\ell$, all four wavelet bands for the HOS

Bias grows with survey area (more area, smaller errors, higher sensitivity). At Stage IV (~14,000 deg2): PS 2.2 sigma, peaks 3.6, l1 3.6. At full sky: PS ~3.5 sigma, both HOS above 6. IMPORTANT: all of this is at FULL MAP RESOLUTION - lmax 1024 for the power spectrum, every wavelet band for the HOS - i.e. no scale cuts yet. That is the point: this is what you get if you use everything the maps offer. HOS are more sensitive than C_l because they live on the contaminated small scales.

§1 Buying back an unbiased inference costs $C_\ell$ most of its multipole range, and the starlet its finest band

  • Criterion: bring the baryonic bias below 0.3$\sigma$
  • $C_\ell$ takes a sliding cut, tuned to what is safe at each area: $\ell_{\rm max} = 860$ at 2,000 deg² falling to 340 at full sky
  • The starlet concentrates the contamination in its finest band, so dropping $j=1$ suffices at every area
$C_\ell$→a cut tuned to each area
starlet→whole bands only, so we cut more than we need
Criterion: baryonic bias below 0.3 sigma. Be careful to be fair here. The power spectrum gets a SLIDING lmax - it can be tuned to keep exactly the multipoles that are safe at each area, from 860 at 2,000 down to 340 at full sky, which is a large fraction of the range removed but it is removed precisely. The starlet gets a different kind of cut: the contamination concentrates in the finest band, so dropping j=1 is enough at every area - but because the bands are dyadic, whole-band removal is the ONLY cut available. That is a blunt instrument: at the smaller footprints it certainly throws away uncontaminated quasi-linear information that a finer filter bank would have kept. So the wavelet cut is not "better", it is coarser and therefore conservative. A sqrt-2 or non-dyadic filter bank would allow an area-tuned cut and recover more. One genuine advantage the HOS do have: they are binned in signal-to-noise as well as scale, and the baryonic response sits in the positive tail, so in principle the contamination can be removed where it sits rather than by removing a whole band. We leave that to future work.

§1 Are HOS still useful?

On baryon-safe scales
  • Starlet $\ell_1$-norm: ×1.8 tighter than $C_\ell$ at Stage IV, ×2.6 at full sky
  • Different degeneracy directions in the $w_0$ planes → HOS always complementary to $C_\ell$
Takeaway
  • HOS are not merely deep-non-linear probes: the signal persists on quasi-linear scales
  • A floor:
    • our whole-band cut is not optimised,
    • better baryon modelling will push the analysis deeper into the non-linear regime, where the HOS gain is larger
On baryon-safe scales the starlet l1-norm constrains x1.80 tighter than C_l at Stage IV and x2.61 at full sky. Be precise about the peak counts: they reach approximate parity with the power spectrum at Stage IV-like areas (x1.07) and slightly exceed it at full sky (x1.17); at the smaller footprints they trail it. Do NOT say they "lose". The reason they trail is largely the cut, not the statistic: the quantised whole-band removal takes out a larger fraction of the peak-count information than the sliding lmax takes from the power spectrum, and that penalty is worst exactly where the power-spectrum cut is loosest. And even at comparable figure of merit they are not redundant - in the planes involving w0 their degeneracy directions differ from the power spectrum, so they add information a joint analysis would use. Takeaway: baryons are a dominant systematic; HOS are robust on quasi-linear scales, not just deep non-linear probes; and everything here is a floor that a finer filter bank or an SNR-space cut would raise.

The claim is usually made against the power spectrum — a benchmark any non-Gaussian summary already beats. Against a strong hand-crafted higher-order statistic, under matched conditions, it hasn't really been tested fairly.

Act 2 opener. This question is GENERATED by the answer to act 1 - do not ask it earlier. SAY: so non-Gaussian information does survive the baryon cut, and the l1-norm reads it well. But that raises the obvious objection, and it is the one I would raise: the summary is the unknown box in the middle of this pipeline, and the field increasingly fills it with a neural network trained to compress the maps - which is often described as optimal. If that is true, why would anyone hand-build a statistic? Take the objection seriously. §2 Read bin by bin, the ℓ1-norm trails the network

the gap
  • The learned summary is 36% ahead in FoM3
  • But they are not reading the same thing: the $\ell_1$-norm one bin at a time, the network all four maps together

SAY: read the result plainly first - the CNN posterior is tighter, FoM3 3326 against 2448, about 36 per cent. Do not editorialise about the network being better, and do NOT yet claim to know why. THEN point at the asymmetry in how the comparison is set up: the lensing kernels overlap while the shape noise is independent, so the bins carry genuinely correlated information; all four maps enter the network's first convolutional layer together, while the l1-norm is computed one channel at a time and only ever sees the one-dimensional marginals. That is an observation about the setup, not yet a diagnosis - it might account for all of the gap, some of it, or none. The next two slides close the asymmetry and measure what happens, which is the honest way to find out.

$$L^{ij}_{ab} \;=\!\!\sum_{\boldsymbol{p}\,\in\,\mathrm{cell}(a,b)}\!\! \tfrac{1}{2}\big(|u_i(\boldsymbol{p})| + |u_j(\boldsymbol{p})|\big)$$

each cell holds the ℓ1 weight of the pixels landing in it

A cross-map collapses each pair into one field before the statistic is taken. The joint ℓ1-norm keeps the whole plane, and needs no new map at all.

SAY: the gap is structural, so there are exactly two places to intervene - the input, or the statistic. ROUTE 1, the obvious one: manufacture the missing channel. For each bin pair, multiply the two maps pixel by pixel; the product is near zero almost everywhere and lights up only where the two bins have coincident structure, so its spatial mean is the zero-lag cross-correlation and its one-point statistics carry the higher joint structure of the pair. Six pairs, six new channels, and the SAME l1-norm runs on each. Built patch-locally, from the patch's own two maps, so a survey observing only that patch could actually form them. IF ASKED: we also test a convolution cross-map, the flat-sky analogue of the harmonic product; it is patch-wide, so its variance sits in a few tens of large-scale modes and it mostly re-supplies two-point information the autos already have. ROUTE 2: leave the four maps alone and change what the statistic reads. At a given scale, every pixel gives the four bins' coefficients at once. The per-bin l1-norm is the |.|-weighted 1-D histogram of each - literally the two curves on the edges of this figure. Lay a fixed 10 by 10 grid on the pair plane instead and sum half of |u_i| plus |u_j| in each cell: that is the joint l1-norm. Ten by ten cells, six bin pairs, four scales, concatenated into the data vector. Summing the absolute value rather than counting pixels is what keeps it an l1-norm rather than a joint PDF estimate. THE POINT, and it is the reason the joint l1 exists: the product map reduces a bin pair to a single derived field BEFORE the statistic is taken; the joint l1-norm never reduces it. CAVEAT if asked: pairwise is the ceiling a patch can populate - a 4-D histogram over K^4 cells against 80x80 pixels is almost everywhere empty, so genuine 3- and 4-bin structure stays with the network. §2 Read the bins jointly and the analytical ℓ1-norm reaches the optimal CNN

The tie holds on every parameter, over 9,000 mock observations.

THE headline. Build it arm by arm: auto-only 2448, plus the product cross-maps 3045, the joint l1 3371, and then the CNN lands at 3326 - on top of the joint l1, not above it. Say precisely what that means and what it does not: a TIE, never a win. The CNN's coverage is mildly conservative, which plausibly accounts for the joint l1 sitting a hair above. And "sufficient as far as we can measure" - both summaries appear to saturate the information the maps make accessible. VMIM trains the network to maximise the mutual information between its summary and the parameters, so it is an estimate of the CEILING, not just another statistic; a hand-built statistic reaching that ceiling is a sufficiency result. Referee aside if pushed: getting the CNN there took an expressive flow (RealNVP, +36%) and a better architecture (resnet18, +6%); the deeper net overfits at 899 cosmologies, so "you trained it wrong" does not hold. The per-mock violins are in backup if anyone wants the population rather than the medians.

§3 Nulling promises localized scale cuts... For HOS it inflates the contours instead

the promise
↓

A fixed angular scale stops mixing redshifts, so the cut can go only where the systematic is

the problem

BNT = a fixed, invertible transform → nothing can have been lost...

SAY: this is the third thing we looked at, and it stands on its own. Nulling - the BNT transform, Bernardeau, Nishimichi and Taruya 2014 - is a linear re-mixing of the tomographic bins that cancels the low-redshift lensing efficiency. Why anyone wants it: standard lensing kernels are broad and overlapping, so one angular scale mixes low-z small scales with high-z large ones, and an angular cut throws away clean high-z information along with the contamination. Null the bins and each transformed field is localized in redshift, so the baryon sensitivity lands in ONE transformed bin and you cut scales only there. That is a genuinely promising way to do scale cuts. CLICK. Applied to a map-based higher-order statistic it backfires: the same mixing correlates the originally-independent shape noise across bins, the noise floor rises, and the contours inflate - worse than standard tomography even with conservative cuts. PLANT, as a minor technical aside: for the power spectrum it IS lossless, PROVIDED you keep the cross-spectra between transformed bins. Do not explain that yet - it pays off on the next slide. Then the paradox, and PAUSE: this is a fixed invertible matrix, the Jacobian cancels in Bayes, the Fisher information is unchanged, the posterior from the full field is identical in both frames - and yet the l1-norm keeps 16 percent. NAME Vinciguerra et al. 2026 (Euclid): they hit the same wall keeping tomographic maps for bin combinations up to quadruplets, and concluded that recovering the SNR is "highly non-trivial".

SAY: so we asked what it takes to get the information back, and ran the same four summaries through the nulled frame. Per-bin l1 keeps 0.16. Add one derived field per pair - the product cross-maps - and it is 0.24, better but nowhere near. The joint l1-norm, the statistic we built two slides ago, keeps 0.72. The CNN, which reads all four channels natively, is essentially untouched by the transform: the measured value is 0.96 +/- the quoted error, consistent with 1, which is why the slide says approximately 1 rather than a false-precision 0.96. Quote it as "no measurable loss", and give the number only if asked. The practical message, and this is the result that matters for anyone planning a nulled analysis: nulling need not cost a higher-order analysis anything, PROVIDED some stage of the pipeline reads the bins jointly. CASH the clause from the previous slide: the power spectrum survives the transform for exactly this reason - auto PLUS cross spectra are closed under C-hat maps to B C-hat B-transpose and so exactly invariant, while the auto-spectra alone keep only the diagonal and are not. The two-point case was the first rung of this same ladder all along. COUNTER-INTUITION worth saying out loud: the nulling is designed precisely to DE-correlate the bins, and joint reading matters more there, not less. HONEST RESIDUAL: the joint l1 keeps 0.72, not all of it - the remainder is genuine three- and four-bin structure that a pairwise statistic cannot reach, and that is the one place the network keeps an advantage. The CNN corner and the calibration of every BNT posterior are in backup.
