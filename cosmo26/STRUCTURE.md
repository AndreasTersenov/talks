## 1. The three questions

**Primary audience.** A broad particle-physics-and-cosmology room — COSMO covers dark matter,
inflation, CMB, GW and neutrinos alongside LSS. Assume they know what cosmic shear is and that
Stage IV is coming. Assume they have never heard of a starlet, the ℓ1-norm, or BNT, and that most
of them will never use any of the three. **What they can carry home is the claim, not the tool.**
Pitch the first third at a CMB theorist.

**The one thing they should remember.**

> A fixed, hand-built wavelet statistic extracts everything an *optimal learned compressor*
> extracts from tomographic weak lensing. It is, as far as we can measure, sufficient — and it
> costs no training at all.

**What should be different afterwards.** That they stop treating a learned summary as
automatically the more informative choice, and treat "we used a neural compressor" as a claim
that now has a hand-built benchmark to beat. Secondarily: that they read the papers, and that
anyone doing a nulled-frame analysis of anything knows the failure mode is the summary, not the
transform.

## 2. ABT

> The prize of Stage IV weak lensing is the non-Gaussian information that two-point statistics
> cannot see, **and** the field's two working assumptions are that baryonic feedback puts much of
> it out of reach and that extracting the rest is a job for a neural network — **but** neither had
> been tested against a strong hand-crafted statistic under matched conditions, **therefore** we

> the most conservative possible baryon treatment, it matches the optimal learned compressor, and
> the single place where it failed turned out to be a property we could build into it.

One "but", covering all three acts. The thread is the same question asked three times: **how much
of the field does a summary actually reach?**

## 3. The three questions — a chain, not a list

The talk asks three questions, but they are **not** stated upfront as a roadmap. Each one is
*generated by the previous answer*, so each opens its own act as that slide's assertion, and all
three are gathered with their answers on the conclusions slide.

| | question, asked at | answered at | answer |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Q1** | S2 — *if we discard every scale feedback touches, do higher-order statistics still buy anything over the power spectrum?* | S5 | **Yes.** The contamination sits in one wavelet band. With no feedback model at all, drop it and the ℓ1-norm still constrains ×1.8 tighter than the power spectrum at Stage IV, ×2.6 at full sky. |
| **Q2** | S6 — *so the ℓ1-norm survives the cut — but is it the right statistic at all?* | S9 | **A wavelet statistic that reads the tomographic bins jointly matches a compressor trained to be optimal** — 3371 against 3326, with no training. |
| **Q3** | S12 — *nulling should fix the baryon problem; applied to higher-order statistics it destroys them, and it is invertible* | S13 | **What survives tracks how jointly the summary reads the bins.** The inflation was the analysis frame, not lost information. |

**Why a chain rather than Fleming's upfront question slide.** These questions are not independent
lines of enquiry that can be listed at the start; each only becomes interesting once the previous
one is answered. Nobody asks "which higher-order statistic is optimal?" until higher-order
statistics have been shown to be usable at all, and nobody asks about the nulled frame until
there is a summary worth putting in it. Stating all three at minute two would flatten a chain
into a checklist and spend the tension early. It would also re-impose the two-papers seam we are
trying to remove, because Q2 would arrive from a different paper rather than out of Q1's answer.

What is kept from the device: the questions are still explicit, still phrased as questions, and
still gathered and ticked at the end (S14), so the audience gets the scoreboard where it counts.

**Q2 and Q3 have the same answer** — read the bins jointly. Q2 discovers the property; Q3 is the
stress test where it turns out to matter enormously.

## 4. Correction to the previous draft, and the resulting spine

The last draft made the ℓ1-vs-CNN comparison the *finale*, on the grounds that it was "a draw and
a slightly flat beat". That was wrong, and it was wrong in a way that cost the talk its headline.

VMIM trains the compressor to maximise the mutual information between its summary and the
cosmological parameters. It is not a rival statistic; it is an estimate of the **ceiling** —
of how much a summary of this dimension could extract at all. A hand-built statistic reaching
that ceiling is not a tie in a contest, it is a **sufficiency result**, and it is strong exactly
because the field has been presenting learned compression as the route to non-Gaussian
information. That claim, plus the fact that sufficiency here comes with no training, no
architecture search, no retraining per survey configuration and an inspectable data vector, is
the most portable thing either paper contains.

So the sufficiency result is the headline act, and the spine returns to the shape you preferred:

**Act 1 — baryons.** Non-Gaussian information survives the most conservative treatment available.
**Act 2 — sufficiency.** The hand-built statistic reaches the ceiling; the network's only edge was
that it read the bins jointly, and we built that in.
**Act 3 — the stress test.** Nulling separates the two summaries dramatically, which both explains
the mechanism behind Act 2 and resolves Act 1's leftover negative result.

What survives from the previous draft: the CNN is a **yardstick, not a rival** — we agree on that,
and the disagreement was only about what it is measuring. It measures *sufficiency* (Act 2), and
it is then reused to measure *where the information went* (Act 3). Introduced once, used twice.

**The ladder, shown in two frames, is the visual spine.** The same four-rung graphic — per-bin ℓ1,
+product cross-maps, joint ℓ1, CNN — appears in Act 2 in the standard frame, where it is nearly
**flat** at the top (2448 → 3045 → 3371, against the CNN's 3326), and again in Act 3 in the nulled
frame, where it is **steep** (0.16 → 0.24 → 0.72 → 0.96). Flat here, steep there. One figure
carries both headline results, and the second showing costs almost no setup.

### 4.1 On mixing the two papers — yes, and here is the payoff

Both papers have a BNT section, and they should be **fused into one block, not presented twice**.
Two reasons, one practical and one much better.

The practical one: nulling is expensive to introduce. Broad kernels, why a fixed angular scale
mixes redshifts, what the transform does. Paying that cost twice in a 15-minute talk is
unaffordable, and the audience does not care which paper a result came from as long as the slide
says. Provenance is a citation line, not a structural constraint.

The real one: **neither paper's BNT story is complete alone, and fused they close a loop.**

Paper I contributes the motivation (feedback is localised in redshift, so nulling should let the
cut go only where it is needed), the two-point result, and the negative result for map-based
higher-order statistics. Paper II contributes the resolution. On its own, Paper I ends on a
failure; on its own, Paper II resolves a puzzle the audience was never given a reason to care
about.

And here is the part worth building the block around. Paper I's two-point result is usually read
as a sanity check — *the transform is invertible, so of course the power spectrum is unaffected*.
But it is only unaffected **if you keep the cross-spectra between transformed bins**; the auto-
spectra alone are not invariant, because they retain only the diagonal of B Ĉ Bᵀ, which cannot be
inverted without the off-diagonals. That is the *same law* as the retention ladder, at second
order: keep the cross-information and the nulling costs you nothing, discard it and it does.

So the two-point case is not a preamble to the ladder — **it is the ladder's first rung**, and the
audience has already seen it before they know what it is. Plant the clause at S10 as an
apparently minor technical caveat; cash it at S12 as the general principle. That is a closed loop,
and it is the most satisfying thing available in either paper.

### Beat notes

**S2 — do not motivate; go straight to what is unresolved.** This room already grants that the
field is non-Gaussian and that higher-order statistics extract more than two-point does. Proving
that premise (the phase/amplitude demo, for instance) would spend a minute on something nobody
disputes. So the opening states the two things that *are* unresolved, which become Q1 and Q2:

The lead line carries the principle the whole talk rests on: *before we trust any summary
statistic we have to quantify how each systematic affects it — and at the contour level, on the
inferred parameters, not just on the data vector.*

The figure is a **deliberately hand-drawn schematic** (SVG turbulence-displacement on the
strokes, and a gentler one on the type, plus an explicit "not to scale" note) so that nobody
mistakes it for a measurement. It shows the information beyond two-point peaking at intermediate
scales and the feedback rising only at the smallest — and then, on one click, the shaded range
that has to be removed grows from **OPTIMISTIC** to **PESSIMISTIC**. That is not decoration: it
poses core question 2 visually. If the contamination reaches only the smallest scales, plenty of
non-Gaussian information survives the cut; if it reaches far enough, nothing worth having is left
and the power spectrum would do just as well. Which of the two is true is the empirical question
Paper I answers.

The two core questions sit below, stacked. Baryonic feedback itself — AGN, supernovae, gas pushed
out of haloes, mimicking a cosmological signal — is spoken, not printed; it is in the notes.

**S3 — the instrument, compressed.** Sum the absolute wavelet coefficients in bins of
signal-to-noise, scale by scale: it uses every pixel, peaks and voids alike, with no discrete
feature to define. SBI gets one strip and one sentence — *no likelihood is written down; a
normalising flow learns the posterior from the simulations.* Resist the pipeline animation.

**S5 — make them feel the stakes.** The bias-vs-area curve shows the problem *arriving*:
tolerable now, intolerable at Stage IV. Say the honest thing — the higher-order statistics are
the **more** biased ones at full resolution. That is the opposite of a sales pitch and it buys
credibility for S6.

**S6 — A1, and it is a floor, not a forecast.** The framing is what makes this result strong, so
say it explicitly: *we assume no model for baryonic feedback at all, and we discard every scale
at which it measurably biases the inference.* That is the most conservative treatment available,
and on what is left the ℓ1-norm still constrains ×1.8 tighter than the power spectrum at Stage IV
and ×2.6 at full sky. Any analysis that models the feedback can only do better — these numbers
are a floor.

Then the caveat that makes it believable: peak counts *lose* to the power spectrum below Stage IV
(×0.46 at 2 000 deg²), and the wavelet cut is conservative because the dyadic bands are coarse.
Return to the question slide, tick Q1.

**S7 — set the yardstick, carefully.** This slide's job is to make the comparison credible
*before* the result lands, because the result invites "you trained it badly". Three fragments:
the same convergence maps feed either summary; both are compressed to ten dimensions and pass
through the same normalising flow; every posterior passes the same coverage battery. Then the key
sentence: **VMIM trains the network to maximise the mutual information between its summary and
the cosmological parameters — it is an estimate of the ceiling, not just another statistic.** And:
3.2×10⁵ patches from 899 cosmologies, with architecture, summary dimension, training budget and
flow family each swept.

**S8 — the gap, and its one cause.** Per-bin, the ℓ1-norm trails on every marginal (2448 against
3326). The reason is structural, not a question of quality: the network mixes all four channels
in its first convolutional layer, so inter-bin information is available to it by construction,
while a statistic evaluated one bin at a time sees only the one-dimensional marginals. This slide
introduces the idea the rest of the talk runs on.

**S9 — the headline. Do not rush.** Build the ladder in the standard frame: per-bin 2448 →
product cross-maps 3045 → **joint ℓ1-norm 3371**, against the CNN's 3326. Then say precisely what
it means and what it does not: *a tie, not a win* — the CNN's coverage is mildly conservative and
plausibly accounts for the difference — and *sufficient as far as we can measure*, in the sense
that both summaries appear to saturate the information the maps make accessible. The claim that
travels: a hand-built statistic reaches the ceiling an optimal learned compressor reaches.

**S10 — what sufficiency buys, and why the result matters beyond lensing.** Given a tie on
constraining power, everything else decides. The ℓ1-norm is **fixed before any simulation is
seen**: no training, no architecture search, no convergence monitoring, nothing that can
overtrain, and no retraining when the survey configuration or the forward model changes. Its data
vector is open to inspection scale by scale and signal-to-noise bin by bin, so an anomaly can be
traced to where it came from, and on large scales its behaviour is analytically predictable. None
of those questions can be put to ten learned coordinates without a dedicated interpretability
study, and seldom answered as directly.

The sharp version, worth saying out loud: a compressor trained to be maximally informative *on
the simulations* cannot distinguish physical features from features of the simulator, and will
exploit whichever carries information. Tick Q2. **If the room photographs one slide, it is this
one or S9.**

**S11 — why you would want the nulling, and the clause to plant.** Broad lensing kernels mean a
fixed angular scale mixes low-z small scales with high-z large ones, so an angular cut discards
clean high-z information along with the contamination. Nulling breaks the coupling and localises
each map in lens redshift. Two frames of the tomography flipbook, then the kernel comparison.

For the power spectrum it does exactly what it promises: lossless at full resolution, and with the
cut applied to the first transformed bin alone the figure of merit improves **×1.4**. Then the
clause, delivered as a minor technical aside: *this requires keeping the cross-spectra between the
transformed bins — the auto-spectra alone are not invariant.* Do not explain it yet. **That it
works here is what makes S12 land, and that clause is what makes S13 close.**

**S12 — the puzzle. Slow down.** The inflated contours for the per-bin ℓ1-norm and peak counts.
Then the contradiction, plainly: this is a fixed invertible matrix; the Jacobian cancels in Bayes'
theorem; the Fisher information is unchanged; the posterior from the full field is identical in
both frames — and yet the ℓ1-norm keeps 16 % of its figure of merit. Vinciguerra et al. (Euclid)
hit the same wall with cross-bin maps up to quadruplets and concluded that recovering it is
"highly non-trivial". **Pause here.**

**S13 — A3, the law, and the loop closing.** Bring back the S9 ladder, now in the nulled frame.
Flat there, steep here: one-dimensional marginals 0.16, one derived field per pair 0.24, full
pairwise distributions 0.72, all four channels 0.96. *What a summary keeps is set by how jointly
it reads the bins.*

Then cash S11's clause: the power spectrum survived the transform for exactly this reason — auto
**plus** cross spectra are closed under Ĉ ↦ B Ĉ Bᵀ and so exactly invariant, while the auto-spectra
alone keep only the diagonal and are not. The two-point case was the first rung of this ladder all
along. Note the counter-intuition too: the nulling was designed to *de-correlate* the bins, and
joint reading matters more there, not less. Tick Q3.

**S14 — conclusions.** The question slide, all three answered. Then the honest residual: in the
nulled frame the joint ℓ1 retains 0.72, not 0.96, and the remainder is the genuinely three- and
four-bin structure a pairwise statistic cannot reach — the one place the compressor keeps an
advantage. And the practical message: nulling can be kept as a mitigation for redshift-localised
systematics at no cost in constraining power, provided some stage of the pipeline reads the
tomographic bins jointly.

## 11. Dreaded questions

1. **"Isn't the CNN just undertrained? That's why it ties."** — The one that matters most, because
   it goes at the headline. Architecture, summary dimension, training budget and flow family were
   each swept; the retained configuration is the best of each sweep and the alternatives land
   within seed-to-seed scatter. 3.2×10⁵ patches from 899 cosmologies, the same simulations feeding
   both summaries. Have the sweep slide ready.
2. **"BNT is invertible, so how can anything be lost? Your result must be a bug."** — Nothing is
   lost; the posterior from the full field is identical in both frames. What changes is what a
   *per-channel* summary can reach. Backup 1 has the formal criterion.
3. **"FoM₃ is fragile in a degenerate parameter space."** — Which is why the marginals are quoted
   alongside it and the population distribution is shown rather than a point.
4. **"Would this still hold for a richer field?"** — Honest answer, and it is in the paper: noisy
   κ maps are close to a Gaussian field plus quasi-circular peaks, which is exactly what the
   starlet basis is built for. Where the field is morphologically richer than that, the balance
   should tip back toward the network.
5. **"Intrinsic alignments / photo-z / source clustering?"** — Not in the forward model. Controlled
   methods comparison; both summaries see the same simulations, so the comparison is not
   confounded, but the absolute numbers are not a survey forecast.
6. **"Why 10° patches?"** — Projection residuals fall from 6.3 % at 20° to 1.5 % at 10°, and the
   cross-map construction has to be patch-local to be physically realisable.
