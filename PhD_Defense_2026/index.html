Mass between us and a galaxy bends the light, and distorts the image we see

Image © American Physical Society / Alan Stonebraker; galaxy images from STScI/AURA, NASA, ESA and the Hubble Heritage Team / astronomie.nl.

Same idea as the cone, drawn properly. Light from distant galaxies does not travel through empty space: it passes every mass on the way, and each one bends its path. By the time it reaches us the image is distorted — stretched one way, squashed the other. For one galaxy the effect is about a per cent, and we do not know the shape it started with. But it is COHERENT: neighbours behind the same structure are distorted the same way. So we measure very many galaxies and look for the shared pattern. That is cosmic shear.

Choosing one map means adding an assumption — and that assumption is the method.

I want to be precise here, because the obvious framing is wrong. We just saw that the relation between shear and convergence is exact and inverts in one line — so the difficulty is NOT that we are going the awkward way round. The difficulty is that we never get to invert gamma, because we never observe gamma. Three things stand between us and it. Shape noise: every galaxy has its own intrinsic ellipticity, far larger than the shear we are after, so the shear estimate at any one place is mostly noise, and the inversion amplifies it at small scales. Masks: the operator is non-local — it mixes Fourier modes — so a hole in the footprint leaves whole modes that the data say nothing about. And the mass-sheet degeneracy: shear is blind to a constant added to kappa, so the overall level is simply not measurable. Put those together and you get this: a family of maps, visibly different — one sharp, one noise-dominated, one heavily smoothed — every one of which fits the measurement. [CLICK] So you cannot simply invert. Choosing one means adding an assumption, and that assumption is not a technicality, it IS the method. Everything in this chapter is a different answer to one question: what do you assume about kappa?

Every method solves the same optimisation — they differ in one term

Find the $\kappa$ that stays consistent with the measured shear while remaining physically plausible.

$$\hat{\kappa} \;\in\; \arg\min_{\kappa}\; \underbrace{\tfrac{1}{2}\big\lVert \mathbf{P}\kappa - \gamma \big\rVert^{2}_{\mathbf{N}^{-1}}}_{\text{data fidelity: does it match the shear?}} \;+\; \lambda\, \underbrace{\mathcal{R}(\kappa)}_{\text{regulariser: is it a plausible map?}}$$

  • $\mathbf{N}$ — per‑pixel noise covariance
  • $\lambda$ — data against prior
  • first term identical for all — the difference is $\mathcal{R}$

$\mathcal{R}$ — what each method assumes a mass map looks like

1Kaiser–Squires no $\mathcal{R}$ — invert, then smooth
2Wiener filtering a Gaussian field, known power spectrum
3sparse recovery mostly empty — a few strong wavelet coefficients
4MCALens both — smooth background, sparse peaks on top
5deep learning learn $\mathcal{R}$ from simulations

Kaiser & Squires 1993, Bobin et al. 2012, Lanusse et al. 2016, Starck et al. 2021, Jeffrey et al. 2020, Remy et al. 2023

The frame first, and it is deliberately NOT Bayesian — that comes later, when we infer cosmological parameters. A reconstruction is a fit. You are looking for the map that does two things at once: reproduces the shear you measured, which is the first term, and is not absurd, which is the second. Every method on this slide writes exactly that problem. The first term is the same for all of them — a chi-squared against the measured shear, weighted by the noise. It is just the forward model we already have. So the entire history of mass mapping is a history of the second term: what you are willing to assume a mass map looks like. Lambda is the dial between the two. [CLICK] Kaiser-Squires has no second term at all: apply the exact inverse and smooth, and the smoothing is the only thing holding the noise down. [CLICK] Wiener assumes the field is Gaussian with a power spectrum you fix in advance — technically an l2 penalty. [CLICK] Sparse recovery assumes the opposite kind of map: mostly empty, with a few strong features, which in the wavelet domain means most coefficients near zero and a handful large. That is an l1 penalty, and it is much closer to what a field of peaks actually looks like. [CLICK] MCALens says you do not have to choose — a real convergence field is a smooth Gaussian background WITH sparse peaks on top, so split it and treat each part the way it deserves. [CLICK] And then deep learning stops assuming altogether and learns the term from simulations. That is Part 2. FLAG, if pressed (Starck): yes, several of these have a Bayesian reading — the l2 penalty is a Gaussian prior, the l1 is a Laplace prior, and the whole thing is a MAP estimate. I have the slide underneath if it comes up. But Wiener filtering and sparse recovery were developed as optimisation problems, and I would rather present them as their authors did.

what a proximal step does

$$\mathrm{prox}_{\lambda\mathcal{R}}(v) \;=\; \arg\min_{u}\; \tfrac{1}{2}\lVert u - v \rVert^{2} \;+\; \lambda\,\mathcal{R}(u)$$

MCALens is the only method here that does not treat the field as one thing. It splits it: a Gaussian background, handled by a Wiener filter which needs nothing but the power spectrum, and a sparse non-Gaussian part in the starlet basis — the peaks, which is where the higher-order signal lives. [CLICK] It solves them alternately: fix one, solve the other, repeat until it converges. [CLICK] And each of those solves ends the same way, with a proximal step. Thirty seconds on what that is, because we meet it again later and I would rather you had the picture than the definition. You take a step towards fitting the data and you land somewhere — call it v. It fits the shear better than where you were, and it may be nonsense as a map: noise everywhere, structure the prior would never allow. The proximal operator is the correction. It hands back the nearest map the prior will accept — nearest to v, so you keep the progress you just made, but acceptable, so you do not run away into noise. Move as little as possible, become admissible. That is the whole idea, and the formula says exactly that: stay near v in the first term, obey the prior in the second. What makes it practical is that for the priors we actually use it has a closed form, and in both cases it is a shrinkage. For the Gaussian component it is a Wiener filter: multiply each Fourier mode by its signal-to-noise ratio, so modes you cannot measure get pulled towards zero. For the sparse component it is thresholding: shrink every wavelet coefficient and zero the small ones. Same idea, one per Fourier mode, one per wavelet coefficient — which is why this algorithm is cheap. Down-arrow for the algebra.

[CLICK] Where does the spread even come from — the reconstruction looks deterministic, you put shear in and get a map out. Two sources. [CLICK] But a network's own variance is not a guarantee — neural uncertainties are famously overconfident. So we calibrate: conformal quantile regression, a held-out set, a per-pixel rescaling, and the coverage comes out at the level we claimed. A normalizing flow: a simple distribution pushed through an invertible map

Start from a unit Gaussian and learn an invertible map onto the distribution you want. The density comes along by change of variables:

$$\log q_\phi(x) \;=\; \log p\big(f^{-1}(x)\big) \;+\; \log\left|\det \frac{\partial f^{-1}}{\partial x}\right|$$

  • each layer has a triangular Jacobian, so the determinant is a product down the diagonal — cheap enough to train on
  • the result is flexible, you can sample it, and you can evaluate its density — all three at once

Condition every layer on some data $x$ and it becomes $q_\phi(\theta \mid x)$ — a posterior you can fit, by maximum likelihood.

Flows, and the setup for the SBI slide. The problem: we need a way to represent a probability distribution that is flexible enough to be an arbitrary posterior, that we can draw samples from, and whose density we can actually evaluate. Those three rarely come together — a histogram is not flexible, a GAN samples but gives you no density, an MCMC chain gives you samples but no normalised density either. A flow gets all three. Start from a unit Gaussian, which you can sample and evaluate trivially, and push it through an invertible map. The picture is exactly that: a Gaussian, bent, stretched, bent again, into something that looks nothing like where it started. [CLICK] Because the map is invertible you can carry the density along with it — change of variables, and the Jacobian determinant is what charges the map for the volume it stretches or squeezes. [CLICK] The engineering trick is to build each layer so its Jacobian is triangular: each output coordinate depends only on itself and the ones before it. Then the determinant is a product down the diagonal, and what would be an n-cubed operation becomes linear. [CLICK] So: flexible, samplable, and evaluable. [CLICK] You fit it by maximum likelihood on samples, which is just gradient descent. And the last step is the one that matters for us: condition every layer on data, and instead of one distribution you have a family — a posterior you can fit. That is the next slide.

We cannot write $p(x \mid \theta)$ down — but we can draw from it. So stop evaluating a likelihood and learn the posterior instead.

So: stage one, and this is the physics half. Draw parameters from the prior, run the forward model, keep the pair. A few hundred thousand times. This is the expensive part, and it is the only expensive part. [CLICK] Stage two, the learning half. SAY: this is the third thing we looked at, and it stands on its own. Nulling - the BNT transform, Bernardeau, Nishimichi and Taruya 2014 - is a linear re-mixing of the tomographic bins that cancels the low-redshift lensing efficiency. Why anyone wants it: standard lensing kernels are broad and overlapping, so one angular scale mixes low-z small scales with high-z large ones, and an angular cut throws away clean high-z information along with the contamination. Fourier tells you which scales are there. A wavelet tells you which, and where

  • a wavelet is a function $\psi$ that is compact and oscillates with zero mean, $\int\psi\,\mathrm{d}x = 0$ — those two properties are the whole definition
  • one shape generates a family, $\psi_{a,b}(x) = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt{a}}\, \psi\!\left(\tfrac{x-b}{a}\right)$, and the coefficient $W(a,b) = \int f\,\psi_{a,b}$ asks how much structure of size $a$ sits at $b$
  • a sine has infinite support: perfect resolution in frequency, none in position. A wavelet gives up a little of the first to buy the second
  • for a field made of localised objects — peaks, filaments, voids — that is the right trade. The power spectrum is the Fourier answer; peaks and the ℓ1‑norm are the wavelet one
BACKUP — only if asked. Start from the thing everyone has: Fourier. The basis is sines. Each one is a single, perfectly defined frequency, and each one runs across the entire domain — so a Fourier coefficient tells you which scales are present and nothing whatever about where. A wavelet is the other trade. It is a small wave: it oscillates and then dies away, and it has zero mean. From one such shape you build a family by dilating and shifting, and the coefficient is the overlap between the data and that stretched, shifted copy — how much structure of that size sits at that place. Zero mean is what makes it a detector of change: over a flat region every coefficient is zero. You pay for the localisation with a little frequency resolution, and for a field made of localised objects that is a bargain. Which is the honest one-line summary of this whole part of the talk: the power spectrum is the Fourier answer, peaks and the l1-norm are the wavelet one.

The transform: the same data at every scale at once

  • slide the dilated wavelet across the data at each scale — the result is one band‑pass image per scale, plus a coarse residual holding what is left
  • the starlet version used here is isotropic and undecimated: every band keeps the full pixel grid, so nothing is lost and the reconstruction is exact, $I = c_J + \sum_j w_j$
  • a feature of a given angular size lands in one band — which is exactly what makes a scale cut cheap, and what Part 3 relies on
BACKUP — the practical half. You take the wavelet, stretch it to a scale, slide it across the data, and record the answer at every position: that gives you one band-pass image per scale, plus a coarse residual holding everything larger than the biggest scale you kept. Here it is on a one-dimensional signal made of three wave packets — a small one, a medium one, a large one — and you can see each of them answering in the band whose width matches it. Two properties of the starlet in particular. It is isotropic, which suits a field with no preferred direction, and it is undecimated: we do not downsample, so every band is the same size as the original and the sum of the bands plus the coarse map reproduces the data exactly. On a two-dimensional convergence map it is the same operation — that is the row of band images you saw earlier in the talk. The last point is the one that pays off in Part 3: a systematic confined to small scales lands in one band, so removing it is a matter of dropping that band.

Where most people meet wavelets: images

In two dimensions each level splits into vertical, horizontal and diagonal details, and recurses on what is left.

  • compression: almost all of an image sits in a few large coefficients — keep those, discard the rest. That is JPEG 2000
  • denoising: the same move with a different rule — threshold the small coefficients. That is the proximal step in Part 1
  • ours is isotropic and undecimated instead: a convergence field has no grain and no preferred direction, and we want every scale on the full grid
BACKUP — only if asked, and the place to send a question that starts "but wavelets are an image compression thing". Yes, and this is the picture: the classical layout, three levels of a separable transform on a photograph — the campus, as it happens. Each level splits the image into vertical, horizontal and diagonal detail and recurses on the approximation, which is the small one shrinking into the corner. Two things people do with it. Compression: nearly all of an image lives in a handful of large coefficients, so keep those and throw the rest away — that is JPEG 2000. Denoising: the same operation with a different rule, threshold the small coefficients instead of the many — and that is exactly the proximal step that MCALens uses in Part 1, which is not a coincidence. What we use is a variant: isotropic rather than separable, because a convergence field has no grain and no preferred direction, and undecimated rather than downsampled, because we want every scale on the full pixel grid.

Bayesian reconstruction

  • Mass mapping problem → statistical inference problem
  • Goal: infer most probable value of $\kappa$-field given observed shear data
$$ \underbrace{p(\boldsymbol{\kappa} \mid \boldsymbol{\gamma}, \mathcal{M})}_{\text {Posterior }} \propto \underbrace{p(\gamma \mid \boldsymbol{\kappa}, \mathcal{M})}_{\text {likelihood }} \,\, \underbrace{p(\boldsymbol{\kappa} \mid \mathcal{M})}_{\text {prior }} $$ $\mathcal{M}$: cosmological model
  • Maximum A Posteriori solution: $\hat{x} = \arg \max_x \, \log p(y|x) + \log p(x)$
  • In practice: this is usually solved iteratively by alternating two steps:
    • Move toward better data fit (gradient of likelihood)
    • Enforce the prior using a proximal operator

Proximal operator
  • Acts as a "smart denoiser" by finding the closest solution that satisfies the prior.
  • Example: sparsity prior → proximal operator performs thresholding to enforce sparsity in the solution.

Sparse recovery

  • Decomposes the signal into another domain (dictionary), where it is sparse

  • Implement the wavelet transform → decomposes the signal into wavelet functions (waveforms of limited duration with an average value of zero)
  • Use starlet wavelets → represent well structures resembling the $\kappa$ of a DM halo (positive & isotropic)

  • The application of sparsity prior enforces a cosmological model where the matter field is a combination of spherically symmetric DM halos

MCALens — the algebra

  • Models $\kappa$-field as a sum of a Gaussian and non-Gaussian component $$\boxed{\kappa = \underbrace{\kappa_{\rm G}}_{\text{Gaussian: Wiener filter}} + \underbrace{\kappa_{\rm NG}}_{\text{non-Gaussian: sparse wavelet}}}$$ $$\min _{\kappa_G, \kappa_{N G}}\left\|\gamma-\mathbf{A}\left(\kappa_G+\kappa_{N G}\right)\right\|_{\Sigma_n}^2+C_{\mathrm{G}}\left(\kappa_G\right)+C_{\mathrm{NG}}\left(\kappa_{N G}\right) $$

  • MCA (morphological Component Analysis) performs an alternating minimization scheme:

    • Estimate $\mathbf{\kappa}_{\mathrm{G}}$ assuming $\mathbf{\kappa}_{\mathrm{NG}}$ is known:$$\min _{\kappa_G}\left\|\left(\gamma-\mathbf{A}\kappa_{N G}\right) -\mathbf{A}\kappa_{G} \right\|_{\Sigma_n}^2+C_{\mathrm{G}}\left(\kappa_G\right)$$
    • Estimate $\mathbf{\kappa}_{\mathrm{NG}}$ assuming $\mathbf{\kappa}_{\mathrm{G}}$ is known:$$\min _{\kappa_{NG}}\left\|\left(\gamma-\mathbf{A}\kappa_{G}\right) -\mathbf{A}\kappa_{NG} \right\|_{\Sigma_n}^2+C_{\mathrm{NG}}\left(\kappa_{NG}\right)$$
