§1 Wavelet peaks: a multi-scale peak count via the starlet transform

  • Multi-scale, not single-scale
  • Starlet transform: a map as a sum of wavelet-coefficient images plus a coarse map
  • Processes all scales simultaneously, for efficiency
  • Each band is a different frequency range, so the peak-count covariance is nearly diagonal
Multi-scale peak counting: apply the starlet transform, count peaks per scale. Each band covers a different frequency range, giving an almost diagonal peak-count covariance. Slide adapted from the LAM deck (dark figure).

§1 Higher-order statistic II: the starlet ℓ1-norm

$$ \ell_1^{\,j,i} \;=\; \sum_{u} \left| \mathcal{S}_{j,i}[u] \right| \;=\; \lVert \mathcal{S}_{j,i} \rVert_1 $$
  • Sum of absolute starlet coefficients, per scale and SNR bin
  • A fast, multi-scale measure of the full void and peak distribution
  • Information in all pixels: peaks and voids, the full convergence PDF
  • No discrete-feature definition
The l1-norm generalizes peaks: sum of absolute starlet coefficients in SNR bins, per scale. Uses every pixel, voids as well as peaks, the full PDF, no discrete-feature definition. Our analytical hero. Slide adapted from the LAM deck (dark figure).

  • At 14,000 deg² (Stage IV): $C_\ell$ already shows $\sim 2\sigma$ tension

§1 But could we do better than that? Weak lensing tomography

Credit: Justine Zeghal
On baryon-safe scales the l1-norm already beats the power spectrum 3x. Could we do better by exploiting the tomographic structure, the redshift bins? The sources are sliced in redshift into per-bin convergence maps; the lensing kernels are broad and overlap, so the bins share information. The natural way to push further is to re-mix the bins: the BNT transform (next slide).

§1 The BNT transform localizes each tomographic bin in redshift (a linear nulling)

→
what BNT does · Bernardeau, Nishimichi & Taruya 2014
  • A linear, invertible nulling of the tomographic bins
  • Standard kernels are broad and overlapping, so a fixed angular scale ℓ mixes many physical scales and redshifts
  • BNT nulls the low-z lensing efficiency, localizing each field in redshift (thin lens-z slices), which sharpens the angular-scale ↔ physical-scale mapping for clean scale cuts

Our use here: isolate the low-z, small-scale systematics (baryonic feedback) to specific bins, and cut scales only where needed, instead of discarding data everywhere.

The BNT transform (Bernardeau, Nishimichi and Taruya 2014) is a linear, invertible nulling scheme that reorganizes the tomographic data into redshift-localized fields. Standard lensing kernels are broad and overlapping, so a fixed angular multipole mixes physical scales and redshifts (low-z non-linear with high-z linear). BNT subtracts weighted lower-redshift bins to null the low-z lensing efficiency, de-projecting the signal so each transformed field probes a narrow lens-redshift range; this sharpens the angular-scale to physical-scale mapping and enables targeted scale cuts. It is a general technique (dynamics, geometry, systematics, nonlinear-scale control); we use it here to isolate low-z baryonic feedback. For the power spectrum it is effectively lossless with the full cross-covariance; for map-based HOS it backfires (next slide).

§1 But applied to map-based HOS, BNT inflates the per-bin contours

the hinge
  • BNT is invertible: no information can truly be lost
  • Yet a Euclid forecast (Vinciguerra et al. 2026) still saw inflated BNT contours, even with explicit cross-bin HOS; recovering the SNR is "highly non-trivial"
  • Is the information really lost, or are we just analyzing it wrong?
The natural way to exploit smaller scales: BNT, a linear invertible re-mixing of the bins that nulls the low-z lensing efficiency, so baryon sensitivity localizes to one transformed bin and you cut scales only there (recall the overlapping kernels from S3). In Paper I we tried it; for map-based HOS it backfires: the same mixing correlates the originally-independent shape noise, the noise floor rises, the HOS contours inflate. §1 You can see it in the maps: BNT trades deep signal for amplified, correlated noise

Fold beat. The same linear mixing that nulls the signal correlates the originally-independent shape noise across bins; the noise floor rises and the maps visibly degrade. This is what inflates the HOS contours.

§1 The same maps, noiseless: BNT cleanly redistributes the signal

Without shape noise, BNT is a clean, invertible redistribution of the signal (the deep common mode becomes one shallow map plus thin slices). The contour inflation comes from the correlated noise it introduces, not from any lost signal.

Noiseless companion (backup). On the signal alone, BNT just reorganizes the information: invertible, lossless. Pair with the noisy slide to show the damage is in the noise, not the signal.

§2 The clincher: a frame artifact, not lost information (one rotation recovers ℓ1, 1.06×)

the information was never lost
  • One fixed whitening rotation Q recovers the full no-BNT FoM3 for ℓ1 too (1.06×)
  • The collapse is a per-channel frame artifact: mix the bins, or re-rotate once
  • Confirms the intuition block; closes the Vinciguerra loop

Closes the Vinciguerra loop: their forecast said recovering the BNT SNR for HOS is "highly non-trivial"; here it is, in one fixed rotation. A frames result: a one-point statistic's information content is basis-dependent, and BNT is simply a poor frame for a per-channel statistic.

The decisive diagnostic. One fixed whitening rotation Q = (BB^T)^(-1/2) B recovers the per-channel l1 fully (1.06x). So the collapse is a frame artifact, not lost info; only a channel-mixing (CNN) or whitened frame recovers it. Quantitatively confirms the intuition block (do not re-explain it).
