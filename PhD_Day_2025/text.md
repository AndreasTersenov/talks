Convergence quantifies the isotropic focusing of light bundles caused by the gravitational influence of foreground matter. Mathematically, convergence is expressed as a line-of-sight projection of the 3D matter overdensity:

The equation for convergence \( \kappa(\boldsymbol{\theta}) \) is a fundamental expression in weak gravitational lensing, and it provides a way to connect the observed distortions in galaxy shapes to the underlying matter distribution in the universe. Let’s break it down step by step and provide some intuition:

### The Equation:
\[
\kappa(\boldsymbol{\theta})
= \int_{0}^{\chi_s}\! d\chi\; W(\chi)\,\delta(\chi\,\boldsymbol{\theta},\chi),\qquad
W(\chi)=\frac{3H_0^2\Omega_m}{2c^2}\,\frac{\chi(\chi_s-\chi)}{a(\chi)\,\chi_s}.
\]

---

### Key Components:
1. **Convergence \( \kappa(\boldsymbol{\theta}) \):**
   - This is the projected mass density along the line of sight, weighted by the lensing kernel \( W(\chi) \). It quantifies how much the light from a distant galaxy is focused or magnified due to the gravitational influence of intervening matter.

2. **Line-of-Sight Integral:**
   - The integral sums up contributions from all matter along the line of sight, from the observer (at \( \chi = 0 \)) to the source galaxy (at \( \chi = \chi_s \)).
   - \( \chi \) is the comoving distance, which accounts for the expansion of the universe.

3. **Matter Overdensity \( \delta(\chi\,\boldsymbol{\theta},\chi) \):**
   - This represents the fractional deviation of the matter density from the average density at a given point in the universe. Positive values indicate regions of higher-than-average density (e.g., galaxy clusters), while negative values correspond to underdense regions (voids).

4. **Lensing Kernel \( W(\chi) \):**
   - The kernel \( W(\chi) \) determines how much a given slice of the universe contributes to the observed convergence. It depends on:
     - **Geometry:** The distances between the observer, the lensing matter, and the source galaxy.
     - **Growth of Structure:** The scale factor \( a(\chi) \), which describes how the universe expands over time.

---

### Intuition:
1. **Projection of 3D to 2D:**
   - The convergence \( \kappa \) is essentially a 2D map of the 3D matter distribution. It tells us how much mass is "projected" along the line of sight in a given direction \( \boldsymbol{\theta} \) on the sky.

2. **Weighting by Geometry:**
   - The kernel \( W(\chi) \) peaks at distances where the lensing matter is roughly halfway between the observer and the source galaxy. This is because the gravitational lensing effect is strongest when the light rays pass near massive structures at intermediate distances.

3. **Growth of Structure:**
   - The scale factor \( a(\chi) \) accounts for the fact that the universe was denser in the past. Structures that formed earlier contribute differently to the lensing signal than those forming more recently.

4. **Physical Interpretation:**
   - Imagine the universe as a giant magnifying glass. The convergence \( \kappa \) tells us how much the glass bends and focuses light in different directions. Regions with high \( \kappa \) correspond to areas with a lot of mass (e.g., galaxy clusters), while regions with low \( \kappa \) correspond to voids.

---

"This slide outlines the inference pipeline used in cosmological analyses, which connects observed data to theoretical predictions and ultimately constrains cosmological parameters.

1. **Observed Data (\( \mathbf{d}_{\mathrm{obs}} \)):**  
   The process begins with the data we collect from observations, such as galaxy surveys or weak lensing measurements. These data encapsulate the physical phenomena we aim to study.

2. **Simulations and Theory (\( \mathbf{d}_{\mathrm{pred}}(\boldsymbol{\theta}) \)):**  
   Next, we use simulations or theoretical models to predict what the data should look like for a given set of cosmological parameters \( \boldsymbol{\theta} \). These predictions are essential for comparing theory with observations.

3. **Summary Statistic (\( s(\cdot) \)):**  
   Both the observed and predicted data are distilled into a summary statistic, \( s \), which captures the key features of the data relevant to the analysis. This step reduces the complexity of the data while retaining the information needed to constrain the parameters.

4. **Likelihood (\( p(s_{\mathrm{obs}} \mid \boldsymbol{\theta}) \)):**  
   The likelihood quantifies how well the theoretical predictions match the observed data. It is often expressed as:
   \[
   \mathrm{L}(\boldsymbol{\theta}) \propto \exp\!\Big[-\tfrac{1}{2}\,\Delta s^{\!\top}\,\mathbf{C}^{-1}\,\Delta s\Big],
   \]
   where \( \Delta s = s_{\mathrm{obs}} - s_{\mathrm{theory}}(\boldsymbol{\theta}) \) is the difference between the observed and predicted summary statistics, and \( \mathbf{C} \) is the covariance matrix that accounts for uncertainties and correlations in the data.

5. **Inference (\( p(\boldsymbol{\theta} \mid s_{\mathrm{obs}}) \)):**  
   Finally, using techniques like Markov Chain Monte Carlo (MCMC), we sample the posterior distribution of the parameters \( \boldsymbol{\theta} \), which combines the likelihood with prior information. This step provides the constraints on the cosmological parameters, revealing insights into the universe's composition and evolution.

This pipeline is the backbone of modern cosmological analyses, enabling us to rigorously test theoretical models against observational data and refine our understanding of the cosmos."

"This slide introduces the traditional approach to cosmological inference, often referred to as the '2-point route.' This method has been a cornerstone of weak lensing analyses and involves the following steps:

1. **Measure Galaxy Ellipticities (\( \epsilon \)):**  
   The process begins with measuring the shapes of galaxies, specifically their ellipticities. These measurements capture the distortions caused by weak gravitational lensing, which encode information about the underlying matter distribution.

2. **Compute the 2-Point Correlation Functions:**  
   The next step is to calculate the 2-point statistics, which quantify the spatial correlations in the galaxy shapes. These functions are sensitive to the clustering of matter and the growth of cosmic structures.

3. **Run MCMC with an Analytic Likelihood:**  
   Using the 2-point statistics, we perform Bayesian inference to recover the posterior distributions of the cosmological parameters. The posterior is given by:
   \[
   p(\theta \mid x) \propto \underbrace{p(x \mid \theta)}_{\text{likelihood}} \underbrace{p(\theta)}_{\text{prior}},
   \]
   where \( p(x \mid \theta) \) is the likelihood, describing how well the model fits the data, and \( p(\theta) \) is the prior, representing our initial knowledge about the parameters.

The accompanying animation illustrates the shear correlation process, providing a dynamic visualization of how galaxy shapes are correlated across the sky. This traditional approach, while powerful, relies on simplifying assumptions and analytic likelihoods, which may not fully capture the complexities of real data."
