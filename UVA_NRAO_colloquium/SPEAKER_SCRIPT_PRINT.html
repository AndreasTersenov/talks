Act 2 — Making the map · frames 9–25

frame 9 · the first half · M.1 · 0:17 · 4:45 in

The first half goes from the measured shear to a map of the matter. This is an ill-posed inverse problem, so it depends on the prior we use, and the result needs an uncertainty.

frame 10 · shear and convergence · M.2 · 0:36 · 5:21 in

Lensing changes the image of a galaxy in two ways: the convergence, an isotropic magnification, and the shear, an anisotropic stretch. The convergence is not directly observable, since we do not know the intrinsic size of a galaxy.

▲ The shear is measurable. Intrinsic ellipticities are, to a good approximation, randomly oriented, while the lensing distortion is coherent over neighbouring galaxies. Averaged over many galaxies, the intrinsic part averages out, and the shear remains.

frame 11 · what the convergence is · M.3 · 0:33 · 5:54 in

The convergence is the quantity we want. It is a scalar field, whereas the shear is spin-2, and most of the statistics I will show later are defined on it. It is also directly physical: the projected matter density along the line of sight, weighted by the lensing efficiency. Convergence maps are therefore called mass maps.

This is a mass map I reconstructed from the UNIONS survey.

frame 12 · the same potential · M.4 · 0:11 · 6:05 in

Shear and convergence are both second derivatives of the lensing potential, so in Fourier space one is a linear function of the other.

frame 13 · the relation is exact, the measurement is not · M.5 · 0:37 · 6:43 in

The relation is exact, but the data are not. The shear is estimated from galaxy shapes, which sample the sky irregularly, carry shape noise much larger than the signal, and are missing in masked regions, for example around bright stars.

Mass mapping is therefore an ill-posed inverse problem: many different maps are consistent with the same data.

[CLICK] ▲ Selecting one requires a prior on the map, and the choice of prior is what distinguishes the methods.

frame 14 · Kaiser–Squires · M.6 · 0:34 · 7:16 in

The standard method, used for thirty years, is Kaiser–Squires: the linear inversion applied directly to the measured shear. It has no free parameters, takes one FFT, and is exact for complete, noiseless data.

[CLICK] On real data it amplifies the shape noise at small scales, and the mask leaks into the whole map, since the operator is non-local. In practice the map is smoothed, which removes the small scales.

frame 15 · interferometric imaging · M.7 · 1:03 · 8:20 in

This is the same problem as interferometric imaging. In both cases the data are a linear instrument response to the image we want, plus noise.

For an interferometer, the visibilities are the Fourier transform of the sky, sampled on the uv coverage, plus thermal noise.

[CLICK] For lensing, the shear is the mass map through the shear operator and the mask, plus shape noise much larger than the signal. These panels are a simulation with a real survey mask.

[CLICK] ▲ The direct inverse gives the dirty image, with its sidelobes; for us, the Kaiser–Squires map, with its noise and its leakage at the mask edges. In both cases the solution is regularised with a prior: CLEAN, maximum entropy or sparsity in radio; the Wiener filter, sparsity or MCALens in lensing.

frame 16 · a data term plus a regulariser · M.8 · 0:25 · 8:45 in

Every method can be written as an optimisation with two terms: a data-fidelity term, which measures how well the map reproduces the shear, and a regulariser, which encodes the prior. The data term is common to all methods; they differ in the prior and in how the problem is solved.

frame 17 · MCALens · M.9 · 0:44 · 9:29 in

Among the methods with an explicit, hand-crafted prior, the state of the art is MCALens. It models the convergence as the sum of a Gaussian component, estimated with a Wiener filter, and a sparse non-Gaussian component that captures the peaks. The two are estimated by alternating minimisation.

[CLICK] Each sub-problem is solved with a proximal step: a gradient step towards the measured shear, followed by an operator that projects the result onto what the prior allows. In the method I present next, this operator is replaced by a neural network.

frame 18 · the question · M.10 · 0:32 · 10:00 in

Different priors give different maps, all consistent with the data. Until our study, mass-mapping methods were compared on the quality of the map, its error against the truth in simulations. But the map is an intermediate product; what we want are the cosmological parameters.

[CLICK] For Euclid this is a practical question: does an advanced reconstruction method improve the constraints enough to justify its cost?

[CLICK] The only thing that varies is the reconstruction.

frame 23 · what a method needs · M.15 · 1:02 · 13:19 in

Since the reconstruction affects the constraints, the next step was to develop a better method for a survey like Euclid. This work was led by Hubert Leterme.

Such a method has four requirements. It has to be accurate, down to the small scales where the gain comes from. Flexible: the mask and the noise vary between surveys and within one, so it cannot require retraining for each configuration. Fast: the same reconstruction is applied to the data and to every simulation used in the inference. And it has to provide calibrated error bars, pixel by pixel.

[CLICK] ▲ No existing method meets all four. The model-based methods are flexible but less accurate, or slow; deep networks are accurate, but must be retrained for every mask and noise level.

frame 24 · plug-and-play · M.16 · 1:57 · 15:16 in

Our method, PnPMass, uses the plug-and-play framework. It is an iteration between two steps, starting from an empty map.

[CLICK] ▲ The first is a gradient step towards the data. This step contains the physical model: the shear operator, the mask, and the noise covariance. The result is a noisy map.

[CLICK] The second is a denoiser: a neural network trained once on simulations, which replaces the proximal operator of the prior. It can represent a much more detailed prior than an analytical one. The result is a clean map.

[CLICK] We iterate until the map converges to a fixed point, shown here next to the true map. Because the mask and the noise enter only through the data step, the same trained network applies to any survey configuration.

[CLICK] ▲ Without error bars, the map is of limited use: we cannot assess the significance of individual peaks, or propagate its uncertainty into a downstream analysis. After convergence, a second network predicts the error of each pixel in a single pass, without sampling.

[CLICK] ▲ A network's own error estimate carries no guarantee, so we calibrate it with conformal prediction, on held-out simulations. These panels show the calibrated lower and upper bounds minus the true map: the lower bound lies below the truth almost everywhere, and the upper bound above it, at the nominal ninety-five per cent rate. Act 3 — Reading the map · frames 26–45

frame 26 · the second half · R.1 · 0:14 · 16:24 in

The second half goes from the map to the cosmological parameters: statistics that capture more than the power spectrum, simulation-based inference, and the small-scale systematics that limit both.

frame 27 · the map has to be compressed · R.2 · 0:18 · 16:42 in

A map has of the order of a hundred thousand correlated pixels, too many to compare with theory directly. [CLICK] We compress it into a low-dimensional summary statistic. [CLICK] The choice of statistic determines how much information is retained.

frame 28 · the two-point function · R.3 · 0:38 · 17:20 in

The standard statistic is the two-point function: the correlation of the shear between pairs of galaxies as a function of separation, or, in harmonic space, the power spectrum.

[CLICK] All wide lensing surveys measure it, KiDS, DES, HSC, UNIONS, because it can be predicted analytically, its covariance is well understood, and its systematics have been studied for two decades.

[CLICK] ▲ For a Gaussian field, the power spectrum contains all the information. The late-time density field is not Gaussian.

frame 29 · same power spectrum · R.4 · 0:19 · 17:39 in

Gravitational collapse concentrates matter into haloes and filaments around voids. These two fields have the same power spectrum but are clearly different, and a two-point analysis cannot distinguish them. The information it misses is accessible to higher-order statistics.

frame 30 · the gain · R.5 · 0:13 · 17:52 in

In forecasts, adding higher-order statistics to the power spectrum significantly tightens the constraints. In Euclid, a dedicated group prepares these statistics for the first data release.

frame 31 · wavelets · R.6 · 0:41 · 18:33 in

The statistics I use are built on wavelets. Where Fourier analysis decomposes a signal into sines and cosines, a wavelet decomposition uses localised, oscillating functions, dilated and translated, so each coefficient measures the structure of a given size at a given position.

[CLICK] This suits a mass map, which consists of localised structures with characteristic sizes. Convolving the map with a small wavelet extracts its small-scale structure, and with a large one its large-scale structure, so the map can be analysed scale by scale.

frame 32 · the two statistics · R.7 · 1:12 · 19:45 in

We use an isotropic wavelet transform, the starlet, which decomposes the map into band-pass images, each containing structure of one characteristic size.

[CLICK] This is one band, pixel by pixel: red above the local mean at that scale, blue below.

[CLICK] The first statistic keeps the local maxima and counts them as a function of height: the wavelet peak counts. Peaks trace dark-matter haloes, whose abundance depends strongly on the cosmology.

[CLICK] Peak counts discard the voids and filaments. The second statistic uses every pixel, sorted by value: the histogram of the band.

[CLICK] Each pixel is weighted by its absolute value, so large coefficients dominate, and those near zero contribute little.

[CLICK] ▲ Summing within each bin gives the starlet ℓ1-norm: the voids on the left, the peaks on the right, one curve per band. It is a summary of the one-point distribution of the field in the wavelet domain.

frame 33 · from the summary to the parameters · R.8 · 0:08 · 19:53 in

To infer the parameters from these statistics, with all the uncertainties propagated, we use Bayesian inference.

frame 34 · classical inference · R.9 · 0:36 · 20:29 in

The posterior is proportional to the likelihood times the prior. The classical analysis assumes a likelihood, usually Gaussian in the data vector, which requires a model for the mean and a covariance matrix. For the power spectrum this is standard.

[CLICK] The posterior is then sampled with MCMC. For peak counts or the ℓ1-norm we have neither an analytical model for the mean nor a justified Gaussian likelihood, so we need a different approach.

frame 35 · generative modelling · R.10 · 0:22 · 20:51 in

That approach comes from generative modelling: learning an unknown distribution from samples drawn from it, by fitting a parametric model. Once trained, the model can generate new samples and evaluate the density at any point.

[CLICK] The same class of models underlies modern image generation.

frame 36 · normalizing flows · R.11 · 0:23 · 21:14 in

We use normalizing flows: a sequence of invertible transformations, each parametrised by a neural network, that maps a standard Gaussian onto the target distribution. Because each step is invertible, the density follows from the change-of-variables formula, and the flow is trained by maximum likelihood on the samples.

frame 37 · simulation-based inference · R.12 · 0:33 · 21:47 in

For our statistics the likelihood cannot be written down, but we can simulate: given the parameters, the simulator produces mock data, including their stochasticity. We draw parameters from the prior, simulate, and keep the pairs.

[CLICK] These pairs train a conditional flow, which then models the posterior directly: given the observed data, it returns the posterior. In Euclid, the weak-lensing group is preparing simulation-based analyses of this kind.

frame 38 · how much can a summary keep · R.13 · 0:42 · 22:29 in

We want the most informative summary. The ℓ1-norm outperforms the power spectrum. [CLICK] But beating the power spectrum is "easy". ▲ The question is how close to all of the information in the map a summary can get.

Since networks already perform the inference, a network can also learn the compression. That has costs: it needs a large training set, it is hard to interpret, and a feature present in the simulations but absent from the data can bias it unnoticed. But it provides the reference.

frame 39 · what optimal means · R.14 · 0:29 · 22:58 in

A network compresses each map into a low-dimensional summary, and a flow maps the summary to a posterior. [CLICK] The two are trained jointly, to maximise the mutual information between the summary and the parameters. [CLICK] In principle, the network then retains the maximum information any summary of these maps can carry, and that is the reference we compare against.

frame 40 · the setup · R.15 · 0:15 · 23:12 in

This is the framework we built: the simulation-based pipeline from the maps to the parameters, the analytical statistics, and the neural compressor. The two approaches share everything except the summary.

frame 41 · the gap · R.16 · 0:19 · 23:32 in

The first result: the network's figure of merit is thirty-six per cent higher, so the ℓ1-norm loses part of the information.

[CLICK] But the comparison is not symmetric: the ℓ1-norm, as used so far, ignores a property of these maps.

frame 42 · tomography · R.17 · 0:49 · 24:21 in

That property is tomography. [CLICK] The source galaxies are divided into redshift bins. [CLICK] The most distant bin [CLICK] is lensed by all the matter in front of it, [CLICK] which gives its convergence map. [CLICK] A nearer bin [CLICK] is lensed by a shorter column of matter, [CLICK] and gives another map.

[CLICK] ▲ There is one map per bin, but the maps are not independent: the same structures appear in several of them. How the signal changes from bin to bin locates the matter along the line of sight, and so constrains the growth of structure. A statistic computed on each map separately cannot capture this.

frame 43 · route 1 · R.18 · 0:34 · 24:55 in

For two-point statistics the extension is straightforward: one also correlates different bins. For higher-order statistics there is no direct equivalent, and we tried two approaches. The first builds, for each pair of bins, a new map that encodes their cross-bin structure, for example the pixel-wise product, which is large only where both maps have structure at the same position. The ℓ1-norm is then computed on these maps as well.

frame 44 · route 2, the joint ℓ1-norm · R.19 · 1:06 · 26:01 in

The second approach changes the statistic itself. This is one wavelet scale, in two redshift bins of the same field.

[CLICK] Each pixel has a coefficient in both bins: one point in the plane.

[CLICK] All pixels together form the joint distribution, correlated because the same matter lenses both bins.

[CLICK] The two per-bin ℓ1-norms depend only on its marginals.

[CLICK] ▲ If we shuffle the pixels of one bin, the marginals are unchanged, but the joint distribution is not. A per-bin statistic cannot detect the cross-bin structure.

[CLICK] We therefore bin the joint distribution on a grid,

[CLICK] and sum the ℓ1 weights of the pixels in each cell. This is the joint ℓ1-norm. The diagonal cells correspond to pixels where both maps have similar amplitude; the off-diagonal cells, to pixels where one is large and the other small.

frame 45 · the answer · R.20 · 0:30 · 26:31 in

Same maps, same flow, four summaries. The per-bin ℓ1-norm. [CLICK] With the product maps added. [CLICK] The joint ℓ1-norm. [CLICK] And the network, which falls almost exactly on the joint ℓ1-norm.

▲ Since the network is trained to be information-optimal, the joint ℓ1-norm retains essentially all the cosmological information in these maps. It requires no training, and each of its terms has a direct interpretation.

frame 46 · the scales of the signal and of the systematics · F.1 · 0:51 · 27:22 in

So far the analysis was idealised. Real data contain systematic effects: physical or observational effects that are not in the model, and that bias the result if they are not accounted for.

▲ The non-Gaussian information and most of these systematics are on the same small scales. Where a systematic cannot be modelled reliably, the conservative option is to remove those scales.

[CLICK] In the optimistic case, only the smallest scales are affected, and most of the non-Gaussian information survives. [CLICK] In the pessimistic case, the contamination extends to larger scales, and after the cut the power spectrum performs as well as the higher-order statistics.

The dominant astrophysical systematic on small scales is baryonic feedback: energy from active galactic nuclei and supernovae expels gas from haloes and redistributes the matter on small scales. This is the Illustris simulation: the dark matter, and the gas heated by feedback.

▲ For galaxy evolution, feedback is a central subject of study. For this analysis it is a systematic we cannot model reliably: the simulations we can afford for inference are dark-matter only, and the feedback models disagree with one another.

[CLICK] This raises two questions. How much does unmodelled feedback bias the higher-order statistics, as survey areas reach Stage IV? [CLICK] And once the affected scales are removed, do they still add information beyond the power spectrum?

Act 5 — Close · frame 52

frame 52 · conclusions · C.1 · 1:41 · 32:13 in

To conclude. Stage IV surveys are now operating: Euclid and Rubin are taking data, and Roman has launched. They will measure the shapes of billions of galaxies, and map the matter over a third of the sky. At that statistical precision, the analysis methods and the systematics set the limit.

We need methods that make full use of the data: the choice of mass-mapping method alone changes the figure of merit by a factor of more than two. Learned priors combine well with the physical model: a network trained once, inside the physical model of the measurement, gives accurate maps for any mask and noise level, with calibrated error bars.

Non-Gaussian statistics are needed to extract the full information of these surveys, and interpretable wavelet statistics, read jointly across redshift bins, match an optimal neural network. Small-scale astrophysics, above all baryonic feedback, has to be treated with care, but even on scales unaffected by it the higher-order statistics add information beyond the power spectrum.

▲ The methods are not specific to lensing: learned priors with calibrated uncertainties, and inference beyond the power spectrum, apply to radio imaging, to 21-centimetre cosmology, and to other inverse problems with a known instrument and noise model.

Q&A — the five to rehearse aloud

The questions this room is most likely to ask and least forgiving about. ⚠ marks an answer whose honest core is a limitation: say it plainly, then the next step.

"How is PnPMass different from AIRI or R2D2?" It is the same family: a learned denoiser inside a forward–backward iteration, as in AIRI. What is specific to ours is the spin-2 lensing operator; a single denoiser trained once on white noise over a range of levels, which works for any noise covariance and footprint because of how the gradient step is preconditioned; and per-pixel error bars with a calibrated coverage. (Backup 69–70.)

⚠ "Does the iteration provably converge?" Formally, it converges linearly to a unique fixed point if the step size is below a bound set by the operator, here 0.176, and the denoiser is mean-preserving and non-expansive. The mean is enforced by construction. Non-expansiveness is not enforced, because constraining the spectral norm of the network's Jacobian during training is expensive, so the guarantee is formal. In practice it converges in a few iterations.

⚠ "Your coverage guarantee holds on simulations. What does it guarantee on real data?" Conformal calibration assumes the calibration maps and the data come from the same distribution. If the simulations miss part of the physics, the guarantee does not transfer. That gap is exactly what "trustworthy" has to address next: testing the simulations against the data, and calibration that is robust to such shifts.

⚠ "Which feedback model did you use, and would a stronger one change the conclusion?" One baryonification model, with fixed parameters. Stronger feedback would push the cuts to larger scales and reduce the gain, so the result is conditional on that model. The next step is a range of feedback strengths, or marginalising over the baryonic parameters instead of cutting.

⚠ "Is the network really optimal? A better one might beat the joint ℓ1-norm." The training objective maximises a lower bound on the mutual information, so the network is the best compression we could train, not an absolute bound. We tried several architectures, and a ResNet-18 was best. The maps were also fairly coarse, 7.5-arcminute pixels; at finer resolution a network may gain more.

Q&A — Euclid

Public facts only (PAPER_FACTS.md §9). Months, never days; nothing from Consortium meetings.

"When can people use the data?" Q1, sixty-three square degrees of deep fields, has been public since March 2025. The next release, DR1-Foundation, is due next month: about nineteen hundred square degrees of images, catalogues and spectra. The weak-lensing and clustering products follow in mid-2027, DR2 in 2029 and DR3, the full survey, in 2031. ESA describes these dates as tentative.

"What does Euclid do that Rubin cannot, and the other way round?" From space the point-spread function is small and stable, about 0.16 arcseconds with 0.1-arcsecond pixels, so galaxy shapes are measured with a PSF several times smaller than from the ground. Rubin goes deeper over a larger area, about eighteen thousand square degrees, in six optical bands, which helps the photometric redshifts. The two are complementary, and joint analyses are planned.

"How do you get the redshifts?" Photometrically: Euclid's near-infrared photometry combined with ground-based optical imaging, UNIONS in the north, and DES and later Rubin in the south. The galaxies are then divided into tomographic bins.

"How many galaxies?" The requirement is at least thirty per square arcminute for lensing; over fourteen thousand square degrees, that is of order one and a half billion.

"What limits Euclid's lensing analysis?" Systematics: the PSF model, the calibration of the shear measurement and of the photometric redshifts, intrinsic alignments, blending, detector effects, and on small scales baryonic feedback, the subject of the last part.

"Will higher-order statistics be part of Euclid's cosmology?" Euclid has a group dedicated to them. A public forecast for a DR1-like setup (Euclid preparation, Vinciguerra et al. 2026) found that each of five higher-order statistics, including the ℓ1-norm and peak counts, tightens w0 by about a factor of 2.5 relative to the shear two-point functions. Which statistics enter the official analysis is decided within the collaboration.

"How does Euclid compare with current surveys?" Current lensing surveys cover hundreds to a few thousand square degrees: KiDS about a thousand, DES Year 3 about four thousand, HSC a few hundred. Euclid covers about fourteen thousand, with space-based shapes.

"Euclid also measures clustering. How do the two combine?" Lensing comes from the visible imager; clustering from slitless near-infrared spectroscopy of Hα-emitting galaxies, at redshifts of roughly one to two, and from the photometric sample. Combining lensing, galaxy–galaxy lensing and clustering breaks degeneracies, for example between the growth of structure and the geometry.

"Is the UNIONS map on your slide Euclid data?" No. UNIONS is a ground-based survey of the northern sky, which also provides optical photometry for Euclid's photometric redshifts. The map is from UNIONS's own shape catalogue.

Q&A — radio and imaging (NRAO, ALMA)

"Is there a regularisation weight to tune, like CLEAN's gain and threshold?" No. The step size is set by the spectrum of the operator, and the denoiser takes the corresponding noise level as an input. The strength of the prior is set by the training distribution, not by a hand-tuned weight.

"What is the noise model? White, correlated?" Shape noise is independent between galaxies, so the covariance is diagonal: the ellipticity variance over the number of galaxies in each pixel, and a very large variance in the masked pixels. The gradient step is preconditioned so that the noise the denoiser sees is white, which is why one network trained on white noise works for any covariance. For radio data, correlated image-plane noise would enter through a data term in the visibility domain.

"Does the denoiser invent structure, in the masked regions for example?" Inside the mask the data carry no information, so the map there comes from the prior; the predicted error is correspondingly larger, and the downstream analyses exclude the masked areas. Elsewhere the fixed point has to stay consistent with the measured shear. The calibration is marginal, so the misses concentrate at the peaks.

⚠ "Could conformal calibration work for interferometric images, without a ground truth?" It needs calibration examples with a known truth, so simulated skies passed through the instrument's forward model: the uv coverage and the noise. The guarantee then holds to the extent that the simulated skies are representative. In cosmology we have simulations built for that purpose; for radio, the sky model is the harder part.

"How do you fix the mean, your equivalent of the missing zero spacing?" The map is reconstructed with zero mean; the denoiser's output is mean-centred. The statistics are computed on wavelet bands, which have zero mean, so a constant offset does not affect them.

"What does it cost?" Training takes about a day and a half for the denoiser and two days for the error network, on one GPU, once. A map with its error bars then takes under a minute, and the calibration about two minutes. DeepMass is faster per map, seven seconds, but has to be retrained, about two days, for every new mask and noise level.

⚠ "Does it scale to Euclid's full sky?" The current version works on flat 5×5-degree fields. A spherical version is the next step. (Backup 111.)

"Why not reconstruct from the galaxy catalogue directly?" Binning the shapes into pixels is the analogue of gridding visibilities, and the pixel noise variance follows from the number of galaxies in each pixel. A catalogue-level forward model is possible, at a higher cost.

Q&A — galaxy evolution and theory (GECO, VITA)

"Instead of removing the feedback scales, could you measure feedback with them?" In principle, yes: with feedback parameters in the forward simulations, the same statistics would constrain them. Baryonification is about a thousand times faster than particle-based methods, which makes such simulation suites feasible. That is future work, not a result.

⚠ "The mass-mapping prior was learned from simulations. Does it depend on their feedback model?" Yes, potentially. The PnPMass denoiser was trained on IllustrisTNG maps, so its prior includes TNG's feedback. Testing it on simulations with a different feedback model, and at other cosmologies, is needed before using it on data; the paper names robustness across cosmologies as the first open direction.

"Do the peaks correspond to haloes?" The high signal-to-noise peaks mostly do, to single massive haloes. Lower ones are often projections of several structures along the line of sight, or noise. The statistic uses them as a population, so all of them carry cosmological information without being identified individually.

"Massive neutrinos also suppress small-scale power. Are they degenerate with feedback?" They are, partly. The forecast I showed included the neutrino mass, and higher-order statistics tightened it. In the feedback study the neutrino mass was not varied.

⚠ "Intrinsic alignments are a small-scale effect too. Are they included?" No. They are not in these simulations; forward-modelling them at map level is on the list, with photometric redshifts and source clustering. (Backup 110.)

"Are dark-matter-only simulations adequate after the cut?" By construction, for this baryon model: we cut until the bias, measured on an observation with feedback, is below 0.3 sigma at each survey area.

Q&A — cosmology and the CMB (Simons Observatory)

"What does this say about the S8 tension?" Nothing directly; there is no new measurement here. But one proposed explanation for the lower lensing S8 is stronger feedback suppressing small-scale power, and those small scales are exactly where cosmology and feedback are degenerate for these statistics. (Backup 55, 57.)

"Could you apply this to CMB lensing, or cross-correlate with it?" The same statistics apply to CMB lensing convergence maps, and cross-correlations with galaxy lensing help calibrate the shear and the redshifts. It is not part of this work.

⚠ "In the first study you used a Gaussian likelihood for peak counts, but later you said it is not justified." The first study used an emulator with a Gaussian likelihood and a simulated covariance, standard at the time. All the methods shared it, so the relative comparison holds; the later studies use simulation-based inference.

"Does the choice of mass map matter for the power spectrum?" The power spectrum is measured from the shear directly, without a map. The mass-mapping choice matters for map-based statistics; the first study used peak statistics.

"Isn't 157 per cent suspiciously large?" The figure of merit here is the fourth root of the Fisher determinant over the four parameters, so a factor of two point six corresponds to error bars about one point six times smaller per parameter, on average. It is also a ratio between two chains that differ in a single step, so systematics that would affect an absolute number cancel.

"Can I compare the numbers across your three studies?" No: they use three different simulation suites and survey geometries. Compare relative values within each study.

Q&A — statistics, machine learning, the ISM

"How do you check the posteriors?" Every posterior is tested for coverage on simulated observations with known parameters, with TARP and simulation-based calibration. (Backup 93–94.)

"Your per-pixel intervals: do they give the significance of a peak?" Not directly. They are marginal and pixel-wise, not a joint band over the map, and they do not encode the correlations between pixels that a peak's significance needs.

"Why normalizing flows, and not other density estimators?" They give exact densities and fast sampling, and neural posterior estimation with flows is well-tested. Other estimators would also work.

"How many simulations did you need?" For the comparison with the network, 899 cosmologies, about three hundred thousand patches. For the feedback study, the full suite of two and a half thousand cosmologies.

"How does the ℓ1-norm relate to the scattering transform?" The mean absolute wavelet coefficient in a band is the first-order scattering coefficient. The ℓ1-norm resolves it by amplitude, so it keeps the shape of the distribution; scattering adds second- order terms that couple scales, while the joint ℓ1-norm couples redshift bins.

"Could the same statistics be used on dust, HI or molecular-cloud maps?" Yes. Multiscale statistics of this kind, and scattering transforms, are already used for the interstellar medium and turbulence; the "same power spectrum, different fields" problem is the same one. (Backup 81, on the phases.)

Q&A — anyone

"When will you apply this to Euclid data?" The weak-lensing products are planned for mid-2027. Everything I showed is on simulations, by design, since validating a reconstruction requires the truth. What transfers directly is PnPMass, which is trained once and takes the mask and noise only at inference, and the ℓ1-norm pipeline, which needs the simulations run at the survey's noise level and footprint.

"Will Euclid use these methods?" They are developed with Euclid in mind, and the collaboration has groups working on higher-order statistics and on simulation-based inference. Which methods enter the official analysis is decided within the collaboration.

"How do you know the machine learning does not invent structure?" In the reconstruction, the network is one step of an iteration whose other step enforces consistency with the measured shear. For the inference, the trained flow evaluates a new observation in milliseconds, so we test it on thousands of simulated observations with known parameters and check the coverage of the posteriors.

"Why the ℓ1-norm, rather than the sum of squares?" The sum of squares in a band is a band power, which is two-point information. The ℓ1-norm, binned by coefficient value, retains the shape of the distribution, including its tails, which is where the non-Gaussian information is.

"Why weak lensing rather than galaxy clustering?" Lensing responds to the total matter, so no galaxy-bias model is needed between the observable and the matter field. It is also sensitive to both the geometry and the growth of structure.

"What is the most important result?" That analysis choices treated as neutral are not: the reconstruction method and the summary statistic both change the constraints, and with calibrated methods we can extract more of the information and quantify how far to trust it.
